1. The Site: Kulaksızlar

Kulaksızlar is a single-period Middle Chalcolithic settlement and specialized marble workshop located near the modern village of the same name in the Akhisar district of Manisa province, western Anatolia (central-western Turkey). Situated on a gently sloping hillside at approximately 150 m above sea level on the eastern edge of the Akhisar Plain, the site rests on limestone bedrock. Cultural deposits are thin (20–40 cm), a factor that has both preserved workshop floors and made the site vulnerable to modern agricultural deep ploughing and looting.

Chronology: Radiocarbon dating of samples from workshop contexts firmly places occupation between 4500–4250 BC (Middle Chalcolithic). Earlier surface surveys had suggested a later date; excavation corrected this significantly.

The site was first identified through surface surveys in the mid-1990s by Rafet Dinç. Systematic survey and analysis by Turan Takaoğlu established it as a rare example of a prehistoric specialized craft production center focused on marble. Rescue excavations in 2018 and 2019 (directed under the Manisa Museum and Ministry of Culture) provided the first stratified contextual data, revealing clustered workshop installations (İşlik A, C, D and others).

Notable Architectural Features

  • U-shaped and oval workshops built with single-course stone foundations; superstructures of perishable materials (wattle-and-daub evidence recovered).
  • Internal stone platforms and low benches used as work surfaces.
  • Spatial clustering of production areas with intervening empty zones (suggesting organized activity zones rather than dense domestic settlement).
  • Evidence of year-round occupation (pottery assemblages including cooking and storage vessels, animal bones dominated by cattle ~72%, loom weights, ground stone tools).

Related finds at nearby Dağdere (approx. 30 km distant) and residual Kilia heads at later sites such as Beçin Castle (Medieval layers) and Aphrodisias demonstrate both intra-regional exchange and long-term curation or residual deposition of these valued objects.

2. Inferred Manufacturing Practices & Craft Specialization

Kulaksızlar represents one of the clearest archaeological cases of pre-urban craft specialization in the Aegean–Anatolian world. Production focused on two main product classes:

  1. Kilia-type schematic marble figurines
  2. Conical marble rhyta (pointed vessels with paired suspension lugs)

Operational Sequence (chaîne opératoire)

  • Procurement of fine-grained local or regional marble.
  • Roughing-out of preforms (blocky to roughly anthropomorphic or conical blanks).
  • Shaping by pecking, abrasion, and grinding using stone hammers, pecking tools, and sandstone abrasives.
  • For rhyta: interior hollowing with rotary motion using bow-drills fitted with sandstone or similar bits of graduated diameters (numerous drill bits recovered).
  • Fine detailing of facial features (raised eyes, nose, ears) and anatomical markers (pubic triangle, arm folds) by incision and low relief.
  • Final polishing to achieve smooth, luminous surfaces.

Workshop waste is highly diagnostic: discarded necks (a common breakage point), unfinished heads, body preforms, and conical vessel fragments with rotary striations. Tools include sandstone drill bits, hammerstones, and abrasive slabs. Obsidian from Melos (Greece) and central Anatolia appears on work platforms, indicating long-distance raw material networks even for toolkits.

Specialization criteria met (after Costin 1991): division of production space, high volume, strong standardization of form and proportions, and high technical investment. This is rare for the period and supports the interpretation of full- or near-full-time specialists rather than purely seasonal or household production.

Finished products left the site; almost no complete examples were recovered in the workshops themselves. This implies deliberate removal for exchange, ritual deposition elsewhere, or both.

3. The Kilia-Type Figurine (“Stargazer”)

Named after the type-site near Kilia (Gallipoli peninsula) where the first known example was found in the 19th century, these figurines are highly standardized schematic female (or ambiguously gendered) forms characterized by:

  • Flat, broad torso with arms folded across the chest (often interpreted as holding the breasts).
  • Long cylindrical neck rising from broad shoulders.
  • Oversized head tilted markedly backward so that the face appears to gaze upward (“stargazer” / “star-gazer” nickname used in some museum contexts).
  • Low-relief facial features: almond eyes, triangular nose, small ears.
  • Incised pubic triangle and leg division on the lower body.
  • Overall proportions that emphasize the head and create a sense of upward orientation.

Sizes range from a few centimeters to larger examples (museum pieces often 15–25 cm). The form is so consistent that unprovenanced examples in Western collections can now be confidently attributed to western Anatolian workshops, with Kulaksızlar as the primary known production center.

4. Humanistic & Cultural Insights

Beyond technical production, these objects open windows onto Chalcolithic social and symbolic worlds:

  • Symbolic value and mobility: The wide distribution of both finished figurines and conical rhyta across western Anatolia (and stylistic influence or actual export into the Aegean islands) indicates that these were not ordinary domestic items. They circulated through exchange networks that linked inland workshops to coastal and island communities.
  • Possible ritual or identity roles: The upward gaze, emphasis on the head, and standardized female form invite interpretations related to fertility, ancestral representation, celestial observation, or status display. Their frequent recovery as fragments may reflect intentional breakage in ritual contexts or simply the fragility of thin marble at the neck.
  • Craft knowledge and social organization: Sustained specialized production implies transmission of skill across generations, possible patronage or community investment in specialists, and the existence of demand that justified the labor investment in marble working.
  • Long-term cultural memory: Residual Kilia heads appearing in much later (Bronze Age, Classical, Medieval) contexts at sites such as Beçin suggest that these objects retained value or curiosity long after their original production period—perhaps as heirlooms, found objects, or antiquities.
  • Modern human cost: High market prices for complete or near-complete examples have driven intensive looting at Kulaksızlar and related sites, destroying contextual information and fueling the illicit antiquities trade. The 2018–2019 excavations were partly motivated by the need to document what remained before further loss.

In short, Kulaksızlar and the Kilia tradition illustrate how specialized craft, aesthetic standardization, and long-distance exchange of symbolic objects were already well developed in the mid-fifth millennium BC, centuries before the better-known Early Bronze Age Cycladic figurine traditions.

5. Machine Learning & Computational Relevance

Why this corpus is valuable for AI / ML research

  • Style classification & attribution: Highly standardized yet subtly variable forms make an excellent testbed for supervised and unsupervised classification models (CNN, transformer-based vision models) that learn the canonical proportions of Kilia vs. other Chalcolithic or Early Bronze Age figurines. Provenanced workshop waste provides ground-truth labels for “in-process” vs. finished states.
  • Manufacturing sequence reconstruction: The rich assemblage of preforms, failures, and tools allows training of sequential models or graph neural networks that infer production stages from 2D photographs or 3D scans of fragments—useful for automated chaîne opératoire analysis.
  • 3D reconstruction & generative models: Broken necks and missing heads are common. Generative adversarial or diffusion models conditioned on complete examples and workshop preforms can propose plausible completions of fragments, assisting museum restoration and virtual reunification of dispersed pieces.
  • Provenance & network analysis: Combining petrographic / isotopic data (if obtained) with findspot distribution enables machine-learning approaches to model exchange networks (e.g., gravity models, Bayesian networks, or graph embeddings of sites).
  • Looted vs. excavated discrimination: Surface-collected and auction-house examples vs. excavated workshop material offer a real-world dataset for training detectors of illicit-market material (surface wear, tool-mark statistics, residual soil chemistry proxies).
  • Digital twins & public engagement: High-resolution photogrammetry of the workshop floors, tools, and figurines can feed interactive 3D environments or AR experiences; ML can auto-generate explanatory labels or guided tours.

The combination of a well-documented production center, standardized yet diagnostically variable products, and a history of both scientific excavation and market dispersal makes the Kulaksızlar–Kilia corpus unusually well suited for computational archaeology pipelines.

Concluding Remarks

Kulaksızlar stands as a landmark site for understanding specialized craft production, inter-community exchange, and the creation of highly standardized symbolic objects in the Middle Chalcolithic of western Anatolia. The combination of workshop architecture, tool assemblages, manufacturing debris, and the wide distribution of finished products provides a rare window into both the technical and social dimensions of early specialized craft. Humanistically, the upward-gazing “Stargazer” figurines continue to invite reflection on how prehistoric communities represented the human form, the divine, or the celestial. Computationally, the corpus offers rich opportunities for machine-learning approaches to classification, reconstruction, network modeling, and cultural-heritage protection.

Further archaeometric sourcing of marble, expanded excavation of the remaining workshop clusters, and systematic comparison of all known museum and excavated specimens will refine our understanding of this remarkable production and exchange system.
